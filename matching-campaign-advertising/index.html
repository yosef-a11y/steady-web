Nonprofits & institutions

A match, a giving day, a thirty-six hour campaign with a sponsor covering every dollar: the format works because the deadline is real and the multiplier is real. That same compression is what makes advertising into one hard. A normal donor acquisition campaign has weeks to find its footing. This one has a window that opens, runs, and closes before most of the machinery paid search depends on has finished waking up.

The grant is least useful exactly when you need it

Most organizations running a match already have an Ad Grants account, and the instinct is to point it at the campaign. It will disappoint, for three reasons that all arrive at once.

The grant's allowance is a daily cap rather than a balance you can save and spend. There is no mechanism to hold it back through a quiet autumn and surge it across a campaign weekend. It cannot concentrate, which is the one thing a match needs.

The grant also bids under a ceiling, and donation-intent searches during a campaign are the most contested they get. A bid cap that's survivable in March is simply out of the auction in the hours when everyone is searching your campaign by name.

And the campaign page is usually not on your own website, which collides with the grant's rules about where its ads are allowed to send people. That one is worth checking before you build anything, not after. The mechanics of grant accounts are on the nonprofit Google Ads page, and if yours is already in trouble, grant suspension has its own page.

None of which means the grant is useless. It means the grant does the year-round work and a small paid account does the campaign.

Automated bidding never gets to learn

Google's bid strategies improve by watching conversions accumulate. A thirty-six hour campaign produces almost all of its conversions inside a window too short for that to happen, so a strategy launched cold on campaign morning spends its best hours figuring out what it is looking at.

The work-around is not a clever setting. It is that the account should already have been running, already tracking donations, and already optimizing, for weeks before the window. The campaign is then a budget change and an audience change on a warm account rather than a cold start. An account built the week before a match is an account learning at the most expensive moment of your year.

The audience has to exist before the clock starts

This is the single highest-leverage part of the whole thing, and it is the part most often left until the campaign is announced.

The people most likely to give in a match are not strangers. They are people who have given before, people on the list who didn't open the last email, people who read the campaign page last year and didn't act, people who watched a video about the institution. Reaching them costs a fraction of reaching a cold audience, and they convert at rates cold traffic never will.

But every one of those audiences takes time to assemble. A remarketing list built on the morning of the campaign contains almost nobody. A customer match audience uploaded from the donor file needs to be in place and processed. Video viewers need video that ran. Weeks ahead, not days.

Done properly, by the time the window opens the advertising is mostly re-reaching people who already have a relationship with the organization, which is both the cheapest traffic available and the most likely to give.

The campaign page usually isn't yours

Most matching campaigns run on a third-party platform. That platform handles the donation, the multiplier, the progress bar and the thank-you page, and it is excellent at all of that. It is also, from an advertising point of view, a black box sitting where your conversion tracking should be.

Sometimes you can place your own tag on it and sometimes you can't. Sometimes the thank-you page is on a different domain, which breaks the measurement even when the tag fires. Find out which situation you're in early, because it determines whether the account can optimize toward donations at all or only toward clicks.

Where the platform won't take a tag, the usable fallbacks are its own reporting with source tagging on every link, reconciled against the ad platforms afterward, and a measurable step on your own site before the hand-off. Neither is as good as a real conversion. Both are much better than running blind and calling the click a result.

The multiplier makes every number look wrong

A two-to-one or three-to-one match inflates every efficiency figure the campaign produces, and if nobody decides in advance which number is being reported, the campaign gets compared against a normal month and declared a triumph on arithmetic alone.

Decide up front whether you are measuring money given or money raised including the match, keep reporting in that unit, and do not carry the matched figure into comparisons with ordinary months. The useful question after a campaign is not whether the matched total was large. It is what the advertising cost per dollar actually donated, and how that compares with what the same spend does the rest of the year.

What the advertising is really for

Most of a successful match comes from the list, the board, the sponsor's own network and word of mouth. Advertising rarely drives the bulk of it and shouldn't be sold as though it does.

What it does well in that window is narrower and genuinely valuable: reaching lapsed and lightly engaged people the email no longer reaches, catching the large number of people who see the campaign, intend to give, and don't act immediately, and being present when people search the campaign or the institution by name because someone told them about it. That last one matters more than it sounds. Word of mouth creates searches, and a campaign with no presence on its own name hands them to whatever else ranks.

Cold donor acquisition inside the window is the one thing it is usually bad at. The window is too short and the auction too expensive to introduce yourself to somebody for the first time.

Everyone who clicked the campaign page and didn't donate is identified, warm, and demonstrably interested, and in most organizations that list is thrown away the week after the campaign ends. When we'd tell you not to advertise the campaign

Some campaigns should spend nothing on ads, and saying so in advance is part of the job.

If there is no time left to build audiences before the window, a cold campaign buying expensive traffic in the hardest hours of the year is unlikely to return what it costs. If the platform won't report back in any usable form, you will not be able to tell afterward whether it worked. And if the organization's list and network are small enough that the match depends almost entirely on a handful of known donors, the money is better spent on reaching those people directly than on an auction.

In each case we'd rather say so before the campaign than produce a report afterward.

How an engagement runs

Month to month, with no long-term contract. The ad account, the audiences built inside it and the conversion tracking are yours and stay yours, which matters more here than almost anywhere else, because the audiences assembled for one campaign are the asset that makes the next one cheaper. An agency that keeps them hands you back a cold start every year.
