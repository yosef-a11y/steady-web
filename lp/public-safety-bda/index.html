What a BDA system does

Concrete, steel and Low-E glass block radio. A BDA puts the signal back.

An ERRC system keeps firefighters and police on their radios everywhere inside your building — stairwells, basements, elevator lobbies, the middle of a warehouse floor. A donor antenna on the roof pulls in the public safety signal, the bi-directional amplifier boosts it, and a distributed antenna network spreads it across every floor. Responder transmissions travel back out the same path to dispatch.

Public safety radio only. This is the two-way radio network fire and police carry into your building — not a cellular booster, not Wi-Fi, not carrier DAS. If your problem is dropped mobile calls in the elevator, we are not the right call. If a fire official needs to reach dispatch from your basement, we are.

01 / Donor

Donor antenna

Roof-mounted and aimed at the public safety site serving the property. Aim and isolation decide whether the rest of the system is stable or oscillating.

02 / Amplifier

BDA & battery backup

A UL 2524-listed amplifier in a NEMA-rated enclosure, with 12 hours of secondary power and faults reported to the fire alarm system.

03 / Coverage

Distributed antennas

Coax or fiber runs feed antennas laid out to hit 95% of general areas and 99% of the critical areas responders depend on.

Commissioning

The part that decides whether you pass

Hanging antennas is the easy half. A system passes or fails on how it is commissioned — gain set against a real link budget, isolation measured rather than assumed, and proof that the amplifier is not feeding noise back into the network it is supposed to support.

This is the sequence we run on every system, and the evidence behind it is what goes into the acceptance test package.

Interference

FirstNet Band 14 is the problem most testers miss

Public safety BDAs are commonly built to pass 700 MHz narrowband, 800 MHz public safety, 800 MHz ESMR — and 700 MHz Band 14, which is FirstNet. As FirstNet LTE has rolled out nationwide, it is increasingly common for an AT&T site to put a signal into a building far stronger than the narrowband public safety signal the system is there to amplify.

What goes wrong

A strong Band 14 carrier drives the amplifier hard, costing headroom the narrowband signal needs and pushing the system toward the noise and interference limits it has to stay inside.

Why it hides

A quick sweep can look healthy while the band that actually matters is starved. It surfaces on acceptance day, or worse, in a building that has been signed off for years.

How we handle it

We identify it in the donor survey and filter ahead of the amplifier, per the manufacturer’s guidance — before the equipment is ordered, not after the test fails.

Code requirements

What the codes ask of an ERRC system — in plain terms

Requirements come from the fire code your jurisdiction adopted, the NFPA standard behind it, and FCC rules on signal boosters. Editions and thresholds vary and your local fire official has the final word — but the shape of it is consistent, and this is what we design to.

Typical ERRC system code requirements
RequirementWhat it means for your building
Coverage 95% of general building areas and 99% of critical areas — fire command centers, exit stairs and passageways, elevator lobbies, standpipe connections, fire pump rooms, and anywhere else the fire official designates.
Signal strength A minimum inbound level of −95 dBm across the required coverage area, with a matching outbound level — a responder’s portable has to reach dispatch from inside the building, not just receive.
Audio quality DAQ 3.0 or better, or the equivalent signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio for the technology in use. Bars on a radio are not the standard; intelligible speech is.
Listed equipment Amplifiers and components listed to UL 2524 under the 2021 IFC and installed as listed. A cellular-grade or unlisted booster is one of the fastest ways to get a submittal rejected.
Amplifier behavior Anti-oscillation detection and control circuitry and per-channel AGC, with adequate donor antenna isolation — the protections that stop a system from harming the network it is amplifying.
Secondary power 12 hours of battery backup under the 2021 IFC — 24 hours in some editions and jurisdictions — in a NEMA 3R or 4-type enclosure, monitored.
Monitoring Antenna, amplifier, AC power and battery faults annunciated at the fire alarm system, so a failed system cannot sit unnoticed until the day it is needed.
FCC authorization Signal boosters operate under FCC rules requiring the licensee’s consent for the frequencies amplified, plus FCC-certified equipment — coordination we handle.
Annual testing IFC 510.6.1 requires annual inspection and re-verification, with records kept for the fire official. The obligation sits with the building owner.

Codes referenced: IFC Section 510 · NFPA 1225 Chapter 18, which carries the ERCES provisions previously in NFPA 1221 (still cited directly by many jurisdictions) · NFPA 72 for monitoring and supervision · FCC Part 90.219. Who carries the risk

The obligation lands on the building owner

Occupancy

Radio coverage is commonly a condition of final inspection. A failed or missing test can hold a finished building empty while the CO waits — one of the few compliance items with that much leverage over a schedule.

Ongoing compliance

Once the system is accepted, keeping it operational and inspected is the owner’s duty, not the installer’s. Lapsed annuals show up as violations and, in some jurisdictions, fines.

The real stake

Beyond the paperwork: a responder who cannot reach dispatch from a stairwell or a basement is the failure the code exists to prevent. That is the standard we test against.

BDA and ERRC, answered

A bi-directional amplifier is the core of an Emergency Responder Radio Communication System. A donor antenna on the roof receives the public safety signal from the outside network, the BDA amplifies it, and coax or fiber runs distribute it to antennas throughout the building — carrying responder transmissions back out the same way. Together those parts are what codes and fire officials call a public safety DAS.

Typically a minimum inbound signal of −95 dBm delivering DAQ 3.0 audio quality or better, across 95% of general building areas and 99% of critical areas — fire command centers, exit stairs, elevator lobbies, standpipe connections, fire pump rooms. Your local fire official sets the adopted edition and can designate additional critical areas.

An RF grid test answers it. The building is divided into a grid and signal strength and audio quality are measured at each point on the local public safety frequencies. Any point below the minimum is a fail for that point. Pass the grid and you submit the report and install nothing; fail it and the same measurements become the design basis.

It depends on square footage, how many floors and stairwells need antennas, the construction type, how strong the donor signal is, and what your local fire official requires. Permit fees and plan review times vary by jurisdiction as well. Anyone quoting a firm number before testing is guessing — we give you a real number once the grid test says what the building needs, and if it passes, the answer is nothing.
