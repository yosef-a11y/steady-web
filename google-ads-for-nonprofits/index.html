The Ad Grant

The Google Ad Grant gives eligible 501(c)(3) organizations a monthly allowance of in-kind search advertising, and most nonprofits that have one spend a small fraction of it. That isn't incompetence. The grant runs under rules a paid account doesn't have — a bid cap, an account-wide click-through floor, search only — and those rules punish the exact behavior that free budget encourages.

Free budget is the trap

The instinct with a grant is to use all of it. It's free, the allowance resets monthly, and leaving it unspent feels like leaving money on the table.

So accounts broaden. Generic keywords get added, loosely related terms get added, anything with volume gets added. Spend goes up. Then the click-through rate across the whole account falls below the floor Google requires, and the account is at risk of suspension — caused directly by the effort to spend the money.

The allowance is a ceiling, not a target. A grant account spending a modest amount on genuinely relevant searches is working correctly. One spending far more on vague ones is on borrowed time.

What makes a grant account different

Everything below is a constraint a paid account simply doesn't have:

  • A bid cap. There's a maximum you can bid manually, and in any contested auction that's below the going rate. The way out is conversion-based automated bidding, which is exempt — which in turn means conversion tracking has to be real before anything else can work.
  • Lower auction priority. Grant ads sit below paid ads competing for the same query. You're working with inventory the paid market didn't take.
  • Search only. No Display, no YouTube, no Shopping, no Performance Max.
  • Keyword rules. Single words and overly generic terms aren't allowed, and keywords have a quality threshold to clear.
  • Mandatory geographic targeting, and ads that have to point at your own domain.
  • Activity and performance requirements that have to be met continuously, not once at setup.

Google publishes the current thresholds and they do change, so check their documentation rather than anyone's blog post — including this one — for the exact numbers before you build to them.

Conversion tracking is the whole game

In a paid account, bad conversion tracking makes optimization harder. In a grant account it makes the account unworkable, because automated bidding is the only route past the bid cap and automated bidding needs conversions to learn from.

Most grant accounts track something that doesn't mean anything — a pageview, a click on an outbound link, time on site. What belongs there instead is whatever actually matters to the organization:

  1. A completed contact or intake form, from someone seeking services.
  2. A volunteer application submitted.
  3. A newsletter or email signup, which is the asset you keep.
  4. A donation completed, with its value passed through.

Weight them honestly. If a donation is worth more to you than a newsletter signup, the account should know that, or bidding will optimize toward whichever is easiest to get.

What the grant is actually good at

Nonprofits usually arrive wanting the grant to raise money. It's better at something else.

Donation intent is a narrow slice of search volume, and it's contested hardest at the moments you'd most want it. Meanwhile there are people searching right now for the kind of help your organization provides, for a program like yours in a place like yours, or for a way to volunteer. That demand already exists, it's cheaper, and it's exactly what a search-only, bid-capped account can reach.

Growing an email list through the grant and fundraising to that list later tends to beat fundraising through the grant directly — and the list is yours, which the ad account never is.

Where a paid account fits alongside it

The two aren't alternatives. A grant does search, below the paid results, inside a bid cap. A small paid account does the rest: remarketing to people who already visited, video and Display when a campaign needs reach, and the competitive terms the grant can't win — around a year-end appeal, say, or a time-boxed match.

Run together they split cleanly. Run as substitutes for each other, both disappoint.

If the account is already suspended

Suspension is a policy outcome, not a punishment, and it's reversible. What it isn't is an appeal problem: appealing without changing the account usually returns the same answer, and repeated appeals make the next one harder.

The sequence that works is to find what tripped it — most often the click-through floor, or conversion tracking that isn't recording, or keywords that were never eligible — fix it properly, prune the terms that caused the drift, and only then request review. The account that comes back is usually smaller and considerably more effective than the one that was suspended.

The grant carries a bid cap that keeps you out of competitive auctions unless you're using conversion-based automated bidding, grant ads sit below paid ads in the same auction, and most nonprofits don't have enough relevant search volume in their area to absorb the full allowance. Chasing the whole amount is what gets accounts into trouble, because the only way to spend it is to bid on terms that have nothing to do with the mission.

Usually the account-wide click-through rate falling below the required floor, which is what happens when an account bids on broad, loosely related, high-volume terms to use up budget. Other common causes are conversion tracking that isn't set up or isn't recording anything meaningful, keywords that are too generic or single words, missing geographic targeting, and long stretches with no account activity. Reinstatement means fixing the underlying behavior, not just appealing.

Not on its own. The grant is search-only — no Display, no YouTube, no Shopping — and the bid cap keeps it out of the auctions where competition is real. It's very good at capturing people already searching for the kind of help you provide. For reaching people who aren't searching yet, or for winning contested terms around a campaign or an appeal, a small paid account alongside the grant does what the grant can't.

It's better at finding people who need your services than at finding donors. Donation intent is a thin slice of search volume and heavily contested at exactly the moments you'd want it. Where the grant tends to earn its keep is program discovery, volunteer recruitment and email list growth — which feed fundraising later, through channels you own.
