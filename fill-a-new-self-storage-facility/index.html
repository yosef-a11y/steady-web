Lease-up

A new facility starts at zero, and the market doesn't know it exists

An operating facility has history working for it: a Google Business Profile with reviews, some organic rank, repeat referrals, and a sense of which sizes actually move. A facility that just got its certificate of occupancy has none of that. It has a building, a rate sheet and a debt schedule.

That gap is the whole problem of lease-up. Every stabilized competitor within a few miles already ranks for the searches your renters are making, and will keep ranking while you build the history to compete. Paid search is the one channel that doesn't care how old you are.

Start before you open

The instinct is to switch marketing on once the gate works. That wastes the most useful weeks you have. People looking for storage are usually moving on a timeline, and someone searching six weeks before your opening is someone a competitor is about to rent to.

Running search ads through the back end of construction does two things. It collects a waiting list you can convert the week you can accept move-ins, so you open with units already spoken for rather than at zero. And it tells you, before you have committed to a rate sheet, which sizes the local demand is actually asking for.

The ads have to be honest about it. An ad that implies you are open when you aren't produces angry calls and wasted spend. "Now pre-leasing, opening in March" sets the expectation and still captures the search.

You don't yet know your unit mix

The mix was decided at design, from a feasibility study and whatever the developer had built before. Reality usually disagrees with it somewhere. One size fills in weeks and another sits.

Search data finds that out faster than occupancy does. The queries people run near your address, and the sizes they click on, are a read on local demand you can act on within the first month rather than the first year. Structuring campaigns by unit size from the start is what makes that visible at all; one campaign for the whole facility averages it away.

Rate strategy during fill

Every operator faces the same trade in lease-up: discount to fill faster, or hold rate and fill slower. The usual mistake is treating it as one decision for the whole facility.

A discounted move-in sets the base that future rate increases build on, so a cheap tenant stays comparatively cheap for as long as they rent. That cost is worth paying on a size you have too much of and not worth paying on a size that is already moving. Concessions aimed at specific sizes, adjusted as the picture changes, beat a site-wide special that gives away margin on units you would have rented anyway.

Aggregators are a tool, not a strategy

Nearly every new facility leans on the listing sites during lease-up, and they do fill units. The trade is that they take a cut of the rent and they own the renter — the tenant belongs to the platform, and the relationship, the data and the next rental go with it.

That can be a reasonable price for early occupancy. It stops being reasonable when nobody is comparing it against the alternative. Once you can see cost per move-in from the aggregators next to cost per move-in from channels you control, the question answers itself, and most operators start shifting the balance well before they reach stabilization.

Defend the name you just created

A new facility has one advantage: nobody is searching for it yet. That lasts until the signage goes up and the listings appear, at which point aggregators and nearby operators will bid on your name. Brand search is cheap and it converts better than anything else you will buy, because the person typing it has already chosen you. Being absent from it is handing over the easiest move-ins you have.

Measure move-ins, not occupancy

Occupancy is the number the lender asks about, and it is the wrong number to steer by week to week. It tells you where you are, not what to do next.

The stages worth tracking back into the ad account:

  1. Inquiry or reservation. Free to make, and a real share never arrive.
  2. Call connected and qualified. A lot of storage demand still picks up the phone.
  3. Move-in completed. The only one that pays rent.
  4. Unit size and rate attached. So the bidding learns a 10x20 is worth more than a 5x5.

Cost per move-in broken out by size is the number that actually directs budget during lease-up. It shows which sizes are still expensive to fill, which have turned the corner, and when to stop paying to advertise something that is nearly sold out.

Spend heavier early, then taper

A flat monthly budget is wrong for a facility that is filling. The early months are when you have the most vacancy, the least history and the most to gain from every move-in, because each one starts a tenancy that compounds through rate increases for as long as it lasts.

Pacing against your own occupancy curve rather than the calendar — heavier while there is a lot to fill, lighter as the remaining vacancy concentrates in a few sizes — is unglamorous, and it tends to be worth more than any bidding change.
