Multifamily & apartments

Almost nobody opens Google and types the name of an apartment community they have never heard of. They type where they want to live. That one fact decides whether paid search is a sensible channel for a property or an expensive way to lose to the listing sites, and it is the first thing worth establishing — before a budget, before a campaign, before an agency.

The anchor test

An anchor is something near the property that renters already search by name. It's what turns a vague "apartments in the city" search into one that points at your location specifically.

Anchors that genuinely carry volume:

  • A university or college. Reliable, seasonal, and the searches say so plainly.
  • A hospital or medical campus. Staff relocate on a rolling basis and search the campus name because that's the commute that matters.
  • A large employer. A plant, a corporate campus, a distribution hub. Relocating hires search the employer, not the submarket.
  • A military installation. Constant turnover and a searcher who already knows the base name and nothing else about the area.
  • A named district or neighborhood. Only if renters actually use the name.
  • A transit station or line. In markets where people genuinely plan around it.
  • A landmark or attraction. The Rodeo Drive case: people search proximity to a place they want to be near.

The common failure is assuming the anchor exists because the property's own marketing uses it. A neighborhood name invented by a developer, or a district branding exercise the city paid for, will show up in the brochure and nowhere in the search data. The anchor has to be the name renters type, which is often older, blunter and less flattering than the one in the leasing brochure.

How to test it before you spend

This is a short piece of work and it should happen before any budget is committed.

Look at whether the candidate anchors have search volume attached to rental intent at all, and whether anyone is already bidding on them. Check what the leasing office hears: when someone calls, how do they describe where they want to live? That phrasing is the anchor, and it's sitting in call notes and guest cards already. Check which listing sites dominate the results for those terms, because that tells you what you'd be buying your way past.

If the honest answer is that nothing points at your location, we'll say so. That's a result, not a failed engagement.

When there's no anchor

Plenty of good properties sit in submarkets with no searchable identifier — a generic suburban corridor, a place people describe by drive time rather than by name. Paid search there means bidding on broad apartment terms against the internet listing services, who have more budget, more inventory on the page and better data than any single community will ever have. That is a losing auction and it isn't close.

What works better in that situation is the listing sites themselves, where the searcher already is; paid social, which reaches people who aren't searching yet; and the Google Business Profile, which captures the people who do search the property by name later. None of that is as satisfying as running your own campaign, and all of it is a better use of the money.

Defend your own name against the listing sites

Type your community's name into Google and look at what sits above your own website. It's usually a listing site, sometimes several, bidding on the name of your property.

Someone searching your community by name is at the very bottom of the funnel. They have already decided to consider you. If a listing site intercepts that search, you can end up paying for a lead you would have had for nothing, and paying it to a platform that is simultaneously showing that renter three competing communities. This is the clearest case in multifamily for defending brand terms directly, and it is frequently the cheapest cost per lease in the account.

Tour requests are not leases

Most multifamily accounts optimize toward a form submission, which means they optimize toward people who like filling in forms. The chain from there to revenue is long and leaky: inquiry, tour scheduled, tour actually attended, application started, application approved, lease signed.

The drop-off between scheduled and attended alone is large enough to change which campaigns look good. A source producing cheap tour requests that nobody shows up for is worse than a source producing fewer that do. Where the property management system — Yardi, Entrata, RealPage or whatever the portfolio runs on — can send those later stages back, the bidding can optimize toward signed leases instead of toward form fills. Until it can, cost per lease is an estimate and should be described as one.

Advertise what's actually available

A campaign promoting one-bedrooms while the only vacancy is a three-bedroom is spending money to frustrate people. Availability in multifamily changes continuously, and the ads have to track it: budget weighted toward the floor plans that are actually empty, and pulled back from the ones that aren't.

Concessions make this sharper. Rents and specials move on a weekly or daily cycle under revenue management, and an ad still promising a concession that expired last week is both a wasted click and a bad first impression. Whatever the ads say about price or specials has to be reviewed on the same cadence the pricing itself changes, not on a quarterly campaign review.

Lease-up and stabilized are different problems

A building in lease-up is trying to fill a lot of units quickly, often before there are reviews, photographs of a finished amenity deck, or any brand recognition at all. The anchor matters most here, because it's the only demand that exists before anyone knows the property.

A stabilized community has the opposite problem. It needs a trickle of leases that roughly matches its notice-to-vacate rate, and spending as though it were in lease-up buys applications it can't house. Budget should follow the vacancy and the expiration schedule, which the property already knows and the ad account usually doesn't.
