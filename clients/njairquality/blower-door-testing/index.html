Plain English

What 3.0 ACH50 actually means

We fit a fan into one of your exterior doorways and pull air out of the house. That drops the pressure inside — about the same as a steady 20 mph wind pushing on every wall, window and door at the same time.

Air rushes back in through every gap. ACH50 is how many times per hour all the air in your house gets replaced at that pressure. Under 3.0 and you pass. Around 2.0 to 2.5 is a comfortable pass. Up near 6 and there's a big gap somewhere — usually where the floor framing sits on the foundation wall.

Worked example. A 2,400 sq ft home with 9-ft ceilings holds about 21,600 cubic feet of air. At the 3.0 ACH50 limit, your fan reading has to come in under 1,080 CFM50. We'll tell you where you landed before we pack up — you never wait on a report to find out whether you passed.

The change that catches builders out: under the 2018 code you could skip the test and take a visual air-barrier inspection instead. The 2021 IECC removed that option. For New Jersey permits filed on or after 6 March 2023, the blower door test is mandatory — there is no visual alternative.

The other test on your permit

Duct leakage test (duct blaster test), same visit

The blower door tests the house. A duct leakage test — most people call it a duct blaster test — tests the ductwork inside it. Most New Jersey code jobs need both, and most inspectors hold the permit open until they have both reports.

We tape off every supply and return register, hook a fan to the return, and pressurize the ducts to 25 pascals. The reading is CFM25 — how much air per minute your ducts dump into the attic, the crawlspace and the wall cavities instead of into your rooms.

≤ 4.0 CFM25 per 100 sq ft of conditioned floor area — the NJ limit, rough-in or final
≤ 3.0 CFM25 per 100 sq ft on a rough-in test run before the air handler is installed
Worked example. A 3,200 sq ft house gets 32 × 4 = 128 CFM25 of allowable total leakage. Come in at 96 and you have room to spare; come in at 210 and there's a disconnected boot in the attic — which we'll find while we're there.

Two things that trip people up. If the entire duct system and the air handler sit inside the conditioned thermal envelope, the duct test isn't required at all — we'll tell you that on the phone rather than sell you a test you don't need. And if you've been quoted an 8 CFM25 limit, that came from an earlier New Jersey amendment; confirm which code edition your permit was filed under before you budget against it.

Duct blaster test cost works the same way as the blower door: one flat fee, quoted on the phone. Book both on one appointment and it's one trip, one price, and both certificates in the same email — almost always cheaper than booking them separately, and it's how we schedule most builder work.

Blower door testing, answered

How much does a blower door test cost in New Jersey?

It's a flat fee, and it depends on three things: the conditioned square footage, how many dwelling units you need tested, and whether you want a duct leakage test on the same visit. A single-family home is one number; a ten-unit townhouse block is a different one.

What we won't do is quote you a low number and add fees later. There is no trip charge, no fuel surcharge, no "report fee," and no charge for the first re-test. Call (973) 557-5437 and you'll have the exact price in about two minutes.

Is a blower door test required for new construction in NJ?

Yes. New Jersey adopted the 2021 IECC as its residential energy subcode, mandatory for permits filed on or after 6 March 2023. Every new residential dwelling unit must be tested and must come in at 3.0 ACH50 or lower (detached one- and two-family homes); attached dwelling units may instead demonstrate 0.3 CFM50 per square foot of enclosure area.

The important change: the 2018 code let you substitute a visual air-barrier inspection for the test. The 2021 code removed that option. If your permit falls under the 2021 subcode, the test is not optional.

What happens if my house fails the blower door test?

With most companies: you get a failing report, a bill, and a phone number for someone who might be able to seal it. With us, the sealing materials are already on the truck.

While the blower door is still running we use a thermal camera and smoke pencil to find exactly where the air is coming in, seal the biggest offenders, and run the test again. The first re-test is free. Most homes that fail by a small margin pass before we leave. If the house is a long way off — a missing air barrier, an unsealed attic plane — we'll tell you honestly what has to happen and what it'll take.

How long does a blower door test take?

About 45 to 90 minutes on site for a typical single-family home, including set-up and tear-down. The pressurized measurement itself takes roughly ten minutes — the rest is setting up the fan and walking the house for leaks. Add around 30 minutes if we're running a duct leakage test on the same visit, and add time per unit on multi-family work.

Who is allowed to perform a blower door test in NJ? Can my builder do it?

The test has to be run by an independent third party — not the builder or the contractor whose work is being tested — using calibrated equipment and following the ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380 test standard. Building departments expect the report to carry the tester's certification and the equipment's calibration status.

Our testers are BPI-certified and our fans are kept in current calibration. Your builder can absolutely be on site while we test; they just can't be the one signing the report.

Do I need a duct leakage test as well as a blower door test?

Usually, yes — if the project has new ductwork. New Jersey's limit is 4.0 CFM25 per 100 square feet of conditioned floor area, whether it's a rough-in or a post-construction test (3.0 on a rough-in run before the air handler goes in).

The exception worth knowing: if the entire duct system and the air handler are inside the conditioned thermal envelope, no duct test is required. Tell us how the system is laid out on the phone and we'll tell you whether you need it. We'd rather skip a test you don't owe than bill you for one. More on duct leakage testing →

Is there a specific blower door test form my inspector needs?

Most New Jersey building departments want a signed envelope leakage test report showing the measured CFM50, the calculated ACH50, the conditioned volume used in the calculation, the test standard, the equipment and its calibration date, and the tester's certification — all referenced to the code section your permit was filed under.

That's exactly what we issue, in the format departments across the state already accept, emailed the same day we test. If your township uses its own form or checklist, send it over and we'll complete that too.

Can I rent a blower door and test my house myself?

You can rent one, and for your own curiosity that's fine. But it won't close a permit. Code compliance requires an independent certified tester, a calibrated fan and a signed report — a rental reading in your own name is not something a building department will accept.

It's also easy to get wrong: the conditioned volume you enter, whether dampers and flues were handled correctly, and how the house was configured all move the number enough to turn a pass into a fail.

When in the build should I schedule the blower door test?

For the final envelope test: after insulation, drywall, windows, exterior doors and all penetrations are complete, and the house can be closed up. You don't need finished floors or trim, but every hole has to be in its final state.

Many builders also book us earlier, pre-drywall, as a diagnostic. It costs less to fix a leak you can still see than one behind finished sheetrock — and it means the final test is a formality rather than a gamble.

Yes, and a lot of our work is exactly that. Attached dwelling units can be tested to the 0.3 CFM50 per square foot of enclosure area limit, which is often easier to hit than 3.0 ACH50 on a small unit with a lot of shared wall.
