Amazon advertising

Amazon ads are judged on the wrong number more often than any other channel. A campaign reports its own ACoS, but the spend that matters is the spend that moves total sales, and that shows up in TACoS — advertising cost as a share of everything the brand sells on Amazon, ads and organic together. A campaign at a high ACoS can be the most profitable line in the account if it pulled organic rank up with it, and a campaign at a low ACoS can be worthless if it was only harvesting sales the brand would have made anyway.

ACoS describes a campaign. TACoS describes the business.

ACoS is ad spend divided by ad-attributed sales. It's a campaign-level efficiency measure and it's useful for comparing two targets against each other. It is not a verdict on whether the advertising is working.

TACoS is ad spend divided by total sales on Amazon, including the orders that came in with no ad involved. It answers the question an owner actually has: what is advertising costing us as a share of the business, and is that share going in the right direction?

The two move independently, which is the whole point:

  • ACoS flat, TACoS falling. Ads are holding their efficiency while organic sales grow underneath them. This is the pattern you want, and it looks like nothing at all if you only watch ACoS.
  • ACoS falling, TACoS flat. The account got more efficient at buying sales it was already going to get. Usually this means brand-name terms are carrying the report.
  • ACoS flat, TACoS rising. Advertising is taking over more of the revenue, which means organic is slipping. Something upstream — rank, reviews, price, stock — is going the wrong way.

Neither number knows anything about margin, which is the third thing to hold in view. Two products at the same ACoS are not equally worth advertising if one has twice the contribution margin, and a category where everyone bids hard can be unwinnable at any ACoS a thin-margin item can tolerate.

“ACoS is down, and the revenue keeps climbing – seriously impressive stuff.”

Jack Richter · Bodno · Google review

The listing is the campaign

On Amazon the ad and the product page are closer together than anywhere else. There's no landing page to write, no offer to construct, no form to optimize. Whatever the detail page does with a click is the whole of the conversion.

That makes conversion rate the lever that moves everything else. Amazon rewards a listing that converts with cheaper traffic and better placement, and it quietly rations traffic to one that doesn't. So a bid strategy is not a fix for:

  • Images that don't answer the obvious question. Scale, what's in the box, how it's used, what it's made of. Most categories lose more sales here than anywhere else.
  • A title written for a keyword tool rather than for a shopper deciding between two options.
  • Price that sits outside what the category has trained buyers to expect, with nothing on the page explaining why.
  • Review volume far below the competing ASINs on the same search. Ads can't buy past that gap; they can only pay more for it.
  • Variations split across separate listings, so reviews and sales history are scattered instead of compounding.

Fixing the listing first is usually cheaper than funding the ads that compensate for it.

The three ad types do different jobs

They get budgeted as one pool and reported as one number far too often.

Sponsored Products harvest demand that already exists. Keyword targeting catches the searches, ASIN and category targeting put the product next to the alternatives a shopper is already considering. This is where most of the volume and most of the waste live.

Sponsored Brands buy the top of the result page with a headline or a video, and need Brand Registry. They work hardest on broad category terms and on competitor terms, where the job is to be the first brand considered rather than the cheapest click. Judged on last-click ACoS alone they almost always look worse than Sponsored Products, because that isn't what they do.

Sponsored Display does two separate things that deserve separate budgets: following shoppers who viewed the product and didn't buy, and defending the brand's own detail pages from competitors advertising on them. The second one is uncomfortable to pay for and still usually worth it, because the alternative is letting a competitor advertise on traffic you earned.

Where the wasted spend usually sits

The same handful of things, in nearly every account we look at.

  1. An auto campaign nobody harvests. Auto targeting is a research tool. Its value is the search-term report, and the report is only worth running if the terms that convert get promoted to their own targets and the terms that don't get negated.
  2. Brand and non-brand in the same campaign. People searching the brand name were going to buy. Averaging them with cold category terms produces an ACoS that describes neither, and it hides whichever half is broken.
  3. No negatives. Wrong size, wrong compatibility, wrong intent, the competitor whose name is similar. Every one of those clicks costs the same as a good one.
  4. Match types collapsed together. Broad, phrase and exact on the same keyword in one ad group compete with each other, and the cheap one takes credit for the expensive one's work.
  5. Placement ignored. Top of search converts differently from the rest of the results and from product pages. If all three pay the same bid, one of them is subsidizing the others.
  6. Dayparting and budget caps left on default. A campaign that exhausts its budget by midday is not a cheap campaign, it's an absent one.

The organic flywheel, stated honestly

Ad-driven sales count toward the sales velocity Amazon uses to decide organic rank. Rank brings sales that cost nothing. Those sales sustain the rank. That's the flywheel, and it's real — it's also the part of Amazon advertising most often oversold.

It only turns under conditions:

  • The listing has to convert. Paid traffic that bounces teaches Amazon the opposite of what you want it to learn.
  • Inventory has to hold. A stock-out doesn't pause the flywheel, it unwinds it. Rank built over months can be given back in a week, and getting it back costs more than holding it would have.
  • The spend has to stay on long enough to compound. Turning campaigns off whenever ACoS looks high and on again when sales dip is the most expensive way to run an account, because every restart pays the learning cost again.

Which is why the launch period and the maintenance period aren't the same job. Early on, the advertising is paying for rank and review velocity and the ACoS should be expected to look bad. Later, it's defending a position, and the number to watch is TACoS trending down while total sales hold.

Buy Box and stock set the ceiling

Two mechanics decide whether any of this is possible, and neither is an advertising setting.

If you don't hold the Buy Box, ads on that ASIN generally stop serving — so a price change, a competing seller on your own listing, or a fulfillment problem can take the campaign down without anything changing in the ad console. Accounts get diagnosed as having a bidding problem when what they have is a Buy Box problem.

And stock level should be driving budget. Advertising hard into thin inventory buys a stock-out; advertising nothing into a deep position leaves rank on the table. Budget belongs on the products that can actually be supplied through the period you're buying rank for.

Amazon doesn't replace Google, and Google doesn't replace Amazon

They sit at different places in the same purchase.

Someone searching inside Amazon is already deciding between products in a closed catalog. There's nothing to persuade them of except which item to pick, and the brand gets the order on Amazon's terms: Amazon's fees, Amazon's customer, and little of the data.

Google and Meta reach the part of the journey that happens before that — people working out what to buy, or who have never heard of the brand. The order can land on the brand's own store, where the margin and the customer relationship both stay in-house, or it can land on Amazon, because plenty of shoppers will go there no matter where they first saw you.

That last effect is why the channels can't be judged in isolation. Google spend routinely produces Amazon sales that no report in either platform attributes to it. The number that holds both together is blended: total ad spend across channels against total revenue across channels, with new customer acquisition cost tracked separately from repeat orders. We build the e-commerce side and the Amazon side to be read together for exactly that reason, and if the store's Merchant Center feed is broken at the same time, that gets fixed first — it's the cheapest revenue in the account.

What we look at first

  1. TACoS by product over time, next to total sales. Direction matters more than the level.
  2. Brand terms separated out, so the rest of the account can be judged on its own.
  3. The search-term report, for terms earning promotion and terms earning a negative.
  4. Contribution margin per product, to set what an acceptable ACoS even is per item rather than per account.
  5. The listings themselves, because a conversion-rate fix beats a bid change nearly every time.
  6. Buy Box and stock history, to find performance drops that were never advertising drops at all.
