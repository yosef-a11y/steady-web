Legal & personal injury

Legal is among the most expensive categories in all of paid search, and personal injury is the most expensive corner of it. That isn’t a reason to avoid it — firms willing to fund it properly do very well — but it does mean the budget question has to be settled before anything else, because a campaign funded at half of what the category costs doesn’t produce half the cases. It produces almost none. The first thing worth working out is what a signed case is worth to you, and whether the math still works at what this category actually charges for one.

Say the budget part first

Most agencies will take a legal account at any budget and report on clicks. We’d rather tell you up front what the category costs, because the alternative is six months of spend that was never going to work.

Here’s the honest shape of it. Click prices in personal injury are set by firms that can pay for a case worth a large contingency fee, and they bid accordingly. A smaller firm entering the same auction on the same terms in the same metro is bidding against that number, not against a fair one. You either fund it at a level that buys enough volume to learn from, or you compete somewhere the bidding is thinner.

Both are real options. What doesn’t work is a budget that buys a handful of clicks a day on the hardest terms in the market, which is what most underfunded legal accounts are.

Practice area decides almost everything

“Legal” is not one market. The gap between practice areas is larger than the gap between industries.

  • Personal injury and mass tort are the brutal end. High case value, national firms, lead sellers, and in a major metro the most contested auction you will find anywhere.
  • Criminal defense is expensive but urgent, and urgency helps — the searcher is calling today, not comparing six firms over a fortnight.
  • Family law has volume, a long deliberation period, and far more tolerable click prices than PI.
  • Estate planning, immigration and employment are often genuinely fundable for a small firm, with real search volume and competitors who are not bidding like a national brand.
  • Business and transactional work rarely starts with a search at all, and usually shouldn’t be the first channel.

If the firm handles several of these, the cheapest fundable practice area is usually where to start, not the one with the biggest case value. Learning the account on affordable traffic and then moving into the expensive terms is a far better sequence than the reverse.

Cost per lead is the wrong number

A legal lead is not a case. Between the form and the fee there’s a long sequence, and most of the people in the top of it will never be clients.

The chain that matters:

  1. Inquiry. A call or form. Many are out of area, out of scope, or outside the statute.
  2. Qualified consultation. Someone with a matter the firm would actually take.
  3. Signed retainer. The real conversion, and the one almost no legal account tracks.
  4. Case resolved. Where the fee arrives, often a year or more later.

Two accounts with identical cost per lead can be a long way apart in cost per signed case, and the difference is usually intake rather than advertising. Until signed cases are flowing back into the ad platforms as conversions, the bidding is optimizing toward whoever fills in forms most readily, which is not the same population as people with strong cases.

Case type belongs in that data too. A soft-tissue auto claim and a commercial trucking case are not worth the same, and an account that treats them as one conversion will cheerfully buy more of the cheaper one.

Intake loses more cases than bidding does

This is the part firms don’t want to hear and the part with the most money in it.

Someone who has just been hurt, arrested or served calls more than one firm. The one that gets a competent human on the phone first signs a disproportionate share, and the advertising cannot compensate for losing that race. The usual failures are mundane:

  • Calls to voicemail during business hours, because the person covering the phone is also doing three other jobs.
  • No after-hours coverage, in practice areas where the events that create clients happen at night and on weekends.
  • An answering service that takes a message rather than an intake that qualifies and books.
  • Callbacks measured in days. By then they’ve signed with someone else.
  • No tracked outcome, so nobody knows which source produced the signed cases.

If the phones aren’t covered during the hours the ads run, the ad schedule should be cut back to the hours they are. Buying clicks into an unanswered phone is the most expensive mistake in this category.

Local Services Ads sit above everything else

For firms that qualify, Local Services Ads are worth understanding separately from search, because they work differently in every respect.

They appear above the text ads, they charge per lead rather than per click, and they require verification — license checks and background screening for the firm and its attorneys — before anything runs. That verification requirement is itself a filter, which is part of why the placement converts the way it does.

They are not a replacement for search. Coverage is uneven by practice area and location, lead quality still has to be reviewed, and disputing bad leads is an ongoing job rather than a one-time setup. But for a firm whose economics can’t survive the click prices on standard search, LSAs are frequently the part of the program that does work, and they belong in the plan before the expensive keyword list does.

What you’re actually bidding against

It helps to know who else is in the auction, because they aren’t all firms.

Lead sellers and directories bid on the same terms, capture the inquiry and sell it on — sometimes to several firms at once. They can outbid a single firm because they monetize the same click more than once.

National and regional advertisers are buying brand as much as cases, and their tolerance for cost per lead reflects a portfolio, not a single matter.

Your own name is being bid on by both. A searcher who typed your firm’s name is the cheapest signed case available to you, and that is the traffic most likely to be intercepted. Brand terms cost little because nobody has a better quality score on your name than you do, and they should be reported separately so they don’t flatter the rest of the account.

Compliance is part of the build, not an afterthought

Legal advertising sits under state bar advertising and solicitation rules that vary by state, and Google applies its own policies on top. Required disclaimers, how results and specializations can be described, and what a landing page has to say differ depending on where the firm is admitted.

We’re not the ones who get to interpret those rules. What we do is build the account so that ad copy, extensions and landing pages are easy for the firm’s compliance reviewer to check and easy to change when a rule does — rather than scattering unreviewed claims across a hundred ad variations.

If the budget isn’t there yet

Saying a category is expensive is only useful if it comes with what to do instead. For a firm that can’t fund head-to-head bidding today:

  • Narrow the geography to the few towns where the firm has a genuine reason to win, rather than the whole metro.
  • Narrow the practice area to the matters with the least competition and the best lead-to-signed rate.
  • Start with Local Services Ads and brand defense, which are the two cheapest sources of signed cases in this category.
  • Fix intake first. It costs nothing in media and it raises the return on every channel the firm ever runs.
  • Build the tracking now, so that when the budget does arrive, the account has real signed-case data to bid on instead of starting from zero.

That’s a slower path and it works. What doesn’t is spreading a small budget thinly across the most contested terms in paid search and waiting for it to turn into cases.

What we look at first

  1. Case value and target cost per signed case, by practice area, so the budget question has a real answer.
  2. Lead-to-signed rate, which usually says more about the firm than about the traffic.
  3. Response time and after-hours coverage, measured rather than assumed.
  4. Whether anything is tracked past the form to consultation and retainer.
  5. Brand reported separately, so the growth side can be judged honestly.
  6. LSA eligibility, before spending on the expensive keyword list.
