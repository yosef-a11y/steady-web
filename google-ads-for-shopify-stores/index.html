Shopify stores

Google Ads on Shopify is mostly a product data job. The ad platform decides what to show, when to show it and what to charge you based on the feed it was handed, so a store with a thin feed is buying the wrong impressions no matter how the campaigns are built. The Google and YouTube sales channel will get a catalog into Merchant Center quickly, and that convenience is exactly why the data underneath it usually goes unexamined for months.

What the Google and YouTube channel does, and where it stops

Shopify's official channel connects the store to Merchant Center and keeps products in sync. It handles the plumbing well: products appear, prices update, items that go out of stock stop showing. For a small catalog of simple products that is genuinely enough.

What it does not do is make judgments. It maps Shopify fields onto Google attributes the same way for every store, so whatever is weak or missing in your product records is faithfully copied into the feed. Titles come across as written for the storefront rather than for a search result. Google product categories get guessed. Attributes you never filled in stay empty, and empty attributes are the ones that quietly limit which queries you can appear for.

The practical question is not whether the channel works. It's whether the feed it produces is the feed you'd write if you were writing it by hand.

Variants are where Shopify and Merchant Center disagree

Shopify thinks of a product as one record with variants underneath it. Merchant Center thinks of every sellable variant as its own item, grouped to its siblings by item_group_id. That mismatch is behind a large share of the disapprovals we see on Shopify accounts.

Two things go wrong most often. The first is identifiers: Shopify's barcode field is what becomes the GTIN, and stores that leave it blank, or paste the same barcode onto every size, end up with items Google either rejects or can't match to the product it already knows. The second is near-duplicate variants competing with each other — eight colorways of the same item, all eligible, all bidding, splitting the data eight ways so none of them accumulates enough conversions to be optimized against.

Neither is a campaign problem and neither gets fixed in the campaign.

Price and availability mismatches are usually a setting

Google crawls the landing page and compares it to the feed. When they disagree, the item comes down. On Shopify the disagreement is rarely a bug; it's a configuration the store forgot it turned on.

Sale prices that are scheduled in an app rather than set on the product. "Continue selling when out of stock" left enabled, so the feed says in stock while the page says sold out. Draft and archived products still reachable by URL. Currency and shipping settings that don't match the country the feed is targeting. Each one produces the same symptom — items disappearing with no change to the campaign — and each one has a different cause.

The checkout moved, and a lot of tracking moved with it

Shopify replaced the old editable checkout with checkout extensibility, and tracking that used to be pasted into checkout templates had to move into the Customer Events pixel system. Stores that migrated without revisiting their tags are the ones now reporting numbers nobody trusts.

The two failure modes are opposite and both common. Some stores lost purchase tracking entirely and are optimizing toward add-to-cart, or toward nothing. Others have the native channel's conversion tag and a manually installed tag and a tag manager container all firing, counting every order two or three times, which makes ROAS look excellent and makes the bidding algorithm chase the wrong thing.

Before any spend decision gets made on an account, a test order should go through and the purchase should appear exactly once, with the right value, in the right currency, attributed to the right source. That check takes an afternoon and it decides whether every number after it means anything.

Margin lives in Shopify, and the feed doesn't know about it

Smart Bidding optimizes toward whatever you tell it is valuable, and by default that's revenue. For a store with uneven margins, that is an instruction to go and sell the cheap-to-buy, low-margin products hardest, because they convert most easily.

Shopify records a cost per item. Getting that cost out of Shopify and into the feed as a custom label — margin bands, or contribution margin tiers — is what lets you separate the catalog into campaigns that deserve different targets. It's unglamorous work and it changes the economics of an account more than almost anything done inside the ads interface.

The same data makes a second thing possible: sending a profit value rather than an order value with the conversion, so the algorithm is bidding toward the number you actually keep.

Selling in more than one country means more than one feed

Shopify Markets makes it easy to show a different currency and price by region. Merchant Center needs that expressed as feeds with the right country targeting, tax and shipping settings behind each one. A single feed pointed at several countries will show the wrong price to most of them, and wrong prices are the fastest route to a suspension rather than a disapproval.

If you only sell domestically this doesn't apply. If you have Markets switched on because it seemed harmless, it's worth checking what Google is being told.

What the first month looks like

The order matters, because each step makes the next one meaningful.

StepWhat gets doneWhy first
1. TrackingTest order through checkout, one purchase recorded, correct value and currencyEvery later decision is made on this number
2. FeedIdentifiers, variants, categories, titles, disapprovals clearedDecides what you're eligible to show for at all
3. MarginCost per item mapped to custom labels, catalog split into tiersLets budget follow profit instead of revenue
4. StructureShopping and Performance Max separated by tier, brand traffic handled deliberatelyOnly useful once the inputs above are right

Most stores want to start at step four. Starting there is why the account gets rebuilt a year later.

When Google Ads isn't the right next spend

If your product has no search demand yet — a new category, an invented one, something people don't know to look for — Shopping will find very little to work with, and the honest answer is that demand generation has to come before demand capture. We'd rather say that at the start than bill for six months of discovering it. The same goes for a catalog small enough that the whole thing can be covered by a handful of search terms: that's a job for a tight search campaign, not a Shopping build.
