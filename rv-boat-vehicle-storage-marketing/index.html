RV, boat & vehicle storage

Vehicle storage is a different business that happens to sit on the same lot as the units. The searcher is different, the season runs opposite to self-storage, tenure is far longer, and the thing being sold is measured in feet rather than in a unit size. Most facilities advertise it as a line on the main storage page and then wonder why the highest-rate spaces on the property are the ones sitting empty. It deserves its own campaign, its own budget and its own page.

The season runs opposite to your storage business

This is the single most useful thing to know about advertising vehicle storage, and it's the reason a shared budget quietly starves it.

Self-storage peaks with moving season. Vehicle storage peaks when the season ends — people look for somewhere to put the boat or the RV when they've finished using it, not when they're about to start. The two curves are close to inverted, so a single budget pacing against facility occupancy will be at its thinnest exactly when vehicle demand is at its highest.

There's a second spike that catches operators out: the period right before the season starts, when owners who stored somewhere bad last year go looking for somewhere better. That's the cheapest competitive win in the category, because those searchers already know what they don't want.

Length is the unit of sale

A self-storage renter searches a unit size. A vehicle owner searches a length, and they know theirs to the foot.

That changes the campaign structure. Splitting by length band — the spaces that take a small trailer or a ski boat, the ones that take a typical travel trailer, the ones that take a Class A or a large cruiser — lets budget move toward whatever is vacant, and stops the facility paying for clicks from someone whose rig won't fit anything available.

The practical detail matters here more than in unit storage. A searcher with a forty-foot fifth-wheel wants to know the space is long enough, that they can get in and out without a twelve-point turn, and that the gate and the aisles will take it. If the page doesn't say so, the call is just to ask.

What they're actually choosing between

Vehicle storage is sold on attributes, and most listings mention almost none of them. The ones that decide the sale:

  • Uncovered, covered or fully enclosed, which is the first fork in the decision and usually the biggest rate difference on the property.
  • Surface. Paved versus gravel versus dirt is not a small detail to someone parking something expensive for six months.
  • Access hours and whether you can get out early. Nobody wants to discover the gate opens at nine on the morning they planned to leave at five.
  • Power at the space for a trickle charge, which for a lot of RV owners decides the whole thing.
  • Dump station, wash bay, air and water. Services that turn a parking space into somewhere people choose to come back to.
  • Security that's actually described — fencing, lighting, cameras, gate access — rather than the word "secure".
  • Pull-through versus back-in. Worth stating, and worth charging for.

Each of those is also a search. Someone typing "covered RV storage with power" has told you exactly what they'll pay for, and generic category bidding never surfaces them.

People will drive further for this

Unit storage is a tight radius, because renters go back to their unit. Vehicle storage isn't. Most owners visit a handful of times a year, so they'll travel considerably further for the right space at the right rate than they ever would for a 10x10.

Two consequences. The targeting radius should be wider than the facility's unit-storage radius, often substantially. And the geography isn't symmetrical: boat owners search near the water they use, RV owners search near home but also near the routes and destinations they travel, which is why a facility on the way to somewhere can win business from well outside its normal catchment.

The auction is thinner than self-storage

This is the good news, and it's why the segment is worth the attention.

The competitors aren't the same set. Alongside other facilities you're up against marinas and dry stack operators, RV dealers with a back lot, farms and open fields renting space informally, and the aggregator listings. Fewer of them are running sophisticated paid search, and the national operators who make self-storage auctions expensive are less consistently present.

So clicks generally cost less than they do on unit storage terms in the same market, while the space earns a higher rate and the tenant stays much longer. That combination is unusual, and it's the argument for giving vehicle storage its own budget rather than whatever is left over.

Tenure is the whole economic case

A boat or an RV doesn't move out because someone finished a renovation. It stays while the owner owns it, which can be years, and the move-out usually happens because they sold the vehicle rather than because they found a better rate.

That means the acceptable cost per move-in is higher than unit storage would suggest, for the same reason it is across the rest of the property: what you can pay is set by tenure and by what the rate does over it, not by the first month. The difference is that here the tenure is longer and the turnover work is smaller, so the case is stronger. The broader move-in economics for self-storage apply, just with better numbers.

Seasonal tenancies are the exception worth tracking separately. A six-month winter storage customer and a year-round one are different outcomes, and an account that treats them as one conversion will optimize toward whichever is cheaper to acquire rather than whichever is worth more.

Keep it out of the main facility's budget

Everything above argues for separation, but the scheduling point is the one that costs money fastest.

If vehicle storage shares a campaign and a budget with unit storage, it loses. Unit storage produces more volume at lower cost per click, so an automated bid strategy optimizing across both will reliably spend on the units and report a good average. The high-rate spaces stay empty and the number on the dashboard looks fine.

Separate campaigns, separate budgets, and a pacing curve that follows vehicle seasonality rather than facility occupancy. It's not a sophisticated change and it's usually worth more than any bidding work done on top of it.

What we look at first

  1. Vacancy by length band, so the budget can go where the empty spaces are.
  2. Which attributes the property actually has — power, surface, cover, services, access hours — because those are the searches and most of them are missing from the site.
  3. The radius, which is usually set far too tight by copying the unit-storage geography.
  4. Whether vehicle storage has its own campaign and budget, or is quietly losing an internal auction to the units.
  5. Seasonal pacing against vehicle demand rather than facility occupancy.
  6. Whether move-ins are tracked at all, with space length and seasonal versus annual attached.
