At two facilities or more,
your main competitor is often you.

Multi-location operators

The job changes at the second facility. With one site the question is how many move-ins the budget can produce. With several it becomes an allocation problem: which site gets the next dollar, which sizes are actually empty there, and whether two of your own locations are currently paying to outbid each other on the same search. Most portfolio accounts are a set of single-facility accounts running side by side, which is why they waste money in ways a single site never could.

Your own facilities are bidding against each other

This is the first thing to look for and it's nearly always there in some form.

Two sites eight miles apart, each with a campaign targeting a radius, and the radii overlap. A search in that overlap triggers both. You don't win twice; you win once and pay more for it, because the second bidder in the auction was you. On brand terms it's worse, since both campaigns are highly relevant to a search for your own company name and the auction has no one else in it to discipline the price.

The fix isn't exotic. Geography gets divided rather than duplicated, with each site owning the area it can realistically win and the overlap assigned to whichever facility has the units free. Brand searches get handled in one place rather than in every campaign. None of this is clever, but it has to be done deliberately, and it almost never survives a portfolio that grew one facility at a time.

Budget should follow vacancy, not site size or seniority

The common pattern is a budget split evenly across facilities, or split by revenue, or set by whichever manager asks loudest. All three are wrong for the same reason: they allocate against the size of the business rather than against the empty space.

A site at high occupancy doesn't need acquisition spend. Advertising into it buys move-ins you'd have got anyway, and worse, it fills the sizes that were going to rent themselves while the genuinely empty sizes stay empty. A site with a soft patch in one unit size needs spend pointed at that size specifically, not at the facility in general.

So allocation works off vacancy by site and by unit size, reviewed on the same cadence your rate decisions are. That's the same principle the single-facility page applies to unit mix, scaled up: the portfolio version just has more places to point the money and more ways to point it wrong.

One account or several?

Operators ask this constantly and the honest answer depends on volume rather than on preference.

Automated bidding learns from conversions. Split a portfolio into many small campaigns and each one is learning from a trickle, which makes the bidding worse everywhere. Pool everything into one undifferentiated campaign and you lose the ability to say where the money goes, which is the whole point of running a portfolio.

The workable middle is usually one account with campaigns grouped so that each has enough conversion volume to be optimized against, and location-level reporting underneath that so you can still see each site. Where a facility is in a genuinely different market with different competitors and different rates, it earns its own campaign. Where two are in the same metro, they usually shouldn't have one.

Reporting a regional manager can act on

The portfolio rollup is where problems hide. A blended cost per move-in across eight facilities can look healthy while two sites are badly underwater and two others are being starved.

What's worth building is cost per move-in by site, and within each site by unit size, so that a conversation about budget is a conversation about a specific vacancy rather than about the account as a whole. Reservations are not move-ins, and a portfolio makes that gap easier to miss, because a site with a weak phone or a slow follow-up process will generate reservations at the same rate as a strong one and convert far fewer of them. Tracking that stops at the reservation hides the facility that needs attention.

Acquisition spend and rate management pull on each other

Pushing in-place rates is the fastest way to grow revenue at a stabilized site, and it also produces move-outs. That's a fine trade when you can refill the space, which means the acquisition budget and the rate policy are two halves of one decision rather than two separate ones.

In practice this means knowing, before the increases go out, what it currently costs you to replace a tenant at that site and in that size. A portfolio that raises rates everywhere on the same schedule and advertises everywhere on the same budget will do well at some sites and quietly damage others.

When a site shouldn't be advertised at all

Some facilities in a portfolio should be spending nothing this month, and saying so is part of the job. A site running near full, in a market where the only effect of more spend would be to pull move-ins forward a few weeks, is a site whose budget belongs somewhere else in the portfolio. The same is true of a facility whose demand is entirely driven by a nearby anchor that search doesn't reach.

We'd rather hand back budget from three sites and put it behind the two that need it than report activity everywhere.

How an engagement runs

Month to month, with no long-term contract. The ad account, the conversion tracking and the history are yours from the start and stay yours, which matters more for a portfolio than for a single site, because the data accumulating across facilities is worth something and it shouldn't live with an agency. Reporting goes to the people who actually make the budget call, at the level they make it.
