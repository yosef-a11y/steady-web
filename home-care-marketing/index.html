Home care & senior care

Whether Google Ads works for a home care agency is a question about the territory, not about the agency. Paid search only produces volume where enough people in the service area are actually typing the search, and in plenty of territories they aren’t — not because demand is missing, but because the person who makes the decision hasn’t got to the point of searching yet. Where that’s the case, Meta is the stronger lead channel, and the right answer is usually both channels doing different jobs rather than one channel doing all of it badly.

Check the territory before you fund the campaign

This is the first thing to settle, and it gets skipped constantly.

A home care agency sells inside a radius — a county, a handful of towns, whatever the caregiver drive time allows. The national search volume for "in home care for elderly" is irrelevant. What matters is how many of those searches happen inside your radius in a month, and how many competitors are already bidding on them.

In a dense metro territory, that number supports a real search program. In a thinner one, a Google campaign ends up bidding on the same small set of impressions over and over, the cost per click rises because franchises and lead sellers are bidding too, and the account looks broken when nothing is broken — there just isn’t enough demand expressed as search to spend against.

So the honest sequence is: look at the real query volume in the real radius first, then decide what search is worth. An agency that tells you the territory won't support paid search is more useful than one that takes the budget and reports on impressions.

The person searching usually isn’t the person receiving care

Almost everything about these campaigns follows from this.

The inquiry typically comes from an adult child, often living somewhere else, often within days of something specific happening: a fall, a hospital discharge with no plan, a diagnosis, a parent who stopped driving, a spouse who can’t manage the caregiving alone anymore. They are anxious, they have no vocabulary for the industry, and they are not comparison shopping in any ordinary sense.

Practical consequences:

  • They don’t know the words. They search "someone to help my mom at home", not "non-medical private duty care". Keyword lists built from industry vocabulary miss the people who are actually ready.
  • They confuse the categories. Home care, home health, assisted living and hospice are four different businesses, and the searcher frequently means one and types another. The page they land on has to orient them quickly or they bounce to someone who does.
  • Geography is split. The decision-maker may be in another state while the care happens in yours. Targeting only the service radius can miss the person holding the phone.
  • The timeline is short and then it’s over. A discharge creates a decision window of days. Whoever is reachable in that window usually wins.

Where Google genuinely earns its place

Even in a modest territory, some search is worth owning.

Condition and situation terms convert far better than the generic category: dementia and Alzheimer’s care, 24-hour and live-in care, respite for a family caregiver, post-surgical and post-hospital help, veterans’ benefits questions. These are people with a defined problem rather than a vague worry.

Your own name is worth defending. Referral networks and national franchises bid on agency names, and if you’re paying a network a placement fee for leads, bidding on your own brand is usually the cheaper way to get the same family.

Payment questions bring in serious inquiries and are badly served online. Most non-medical home care is private pay or long-term care insurance; Medicare does not cover ongoing custodial help the way families assume it does, and whether a state Medicaid waiver covers any of it varies by state and by program. Families arrive believing otherwise. A page that answers the payment question honestly, including when the answer is "not through Medicare", earns the call.

What tends not to work is the broad category term bought at scale in a thin market. That’s exactly where the budget disappears.

Where Meta does the work search can’t

Search can only capture demand that has already turned into a query. A large share of home care demand hasn’t yet — the family is worrying about it, arguing about it, putting it off. Meta reaches that family before the search happens, which is why in thin territories it carries the program rather than supplementing it.

What makes it work:

  • Targeting the adult child, not the senior. The audience is people in and around the service area at the life stage where a parent’s decline is live, and the creative has to speak to them rather than to the person receiving care.
  • Creative that names the moment. The ad that works describes a recognizable situation — the calls getting more frequent, a parent not safe alone overnight, a sibling doing all of it. Generic warmth and stock photography of smiling seniors gets scrolled past.
  • Enough friction in the form to filter. An instant lead form with three fields produces a cheap cost per lead and a lot of people who weren’t ready. Adding qualifying questions raises the cost per lead and raises the number of families who actually start care, which is the trade you want.
  • Volume of creative, not one perfect ad. The audience is finite and tires quickly, so the program needs a steady supply of new angles rather than one asset run into the ground.

The channels also need to be judged differently. Meta leads arrive earlier in the decision, so they cost less per lead and take longer to convert. Comparing Meta’s cost per lead to Google’s makes Meta look excellent and then disappointing. Comparing cost per start of care makes both legible.

The only number that matters is a start of care

Form fills are not the product. A home care client isn’t even a fixed amount of revenue — the value is hours per week multiplied by how long they stay, and a four-hour-a-week companion client and a live-in client are not the same win.

So the tracking has to run past the inquiry:

  1. Inquiry to qualified conversation. How many inquiries reach a real intake discussion rather than voicemail.
  2. Qualified to assessment. The in-home assessment is the real commitment point.
  3. Assessment to start of care, with the date, so the lag is visible.
  4. Hours authorized at start, so the campaigns can be optimized toward the clients worth having rather than the cheapest ones.

Feeding that back into the ad platforms — importing the offline conversion with its value instead of counting form submissions — is what stops the algorithm from optimizing enthusiastically toward the wrong families. It’s the same discipline we use to track ABA inquiries through to intake and storage inquiries through to move-in.

Speed to the phone decides most of it

The adult child in a discharge window contacts more than one agency. The one that gets a human on the phone first is usually the one that gets the assessment, and nothing in the ad account compensates for losing that race.

Which makes a few unglamorous things part of the marketing: someone covering the phone during the hours the ads run, a callback measured in minutes rather than the next business day, and after-hours coverage, because falls and discharges don’t keep office hours. If an agency can’t answer evenings and weekends, the ad schedule should reflect that rather than buying clicks into an empty office.

Caregiver recruiting is a different campaign

Most agencies are short on caregivers, and turning down hours for lack of staff is more expensive than any ad budget. Recruiting is a lead generation problem with its own account: commute-radius targeting rather than city lists, pay stated in the ad, a single-role landing page, and tracking that runs through to a start date rather than stopping at an application. It’s the same method we use for BCBA and RBT recruiting, and it should never share a budget with client acquisition — the two compete, and the one with the easier conversion wins the money.

What we look at first

  1. Real search volume in the real radius, to decide whether Google is a lead channel here or a brand-defense line item.
  2. Who is actually inquiring — adult child, spouse, discharge planner, the senior — because the targeting and the copy follow from that.
  3. Response time on existing inquiries, including evenings and weekends.
  4. The payment conversation, since unqualified private-pay inquiries are the most common form of expensive waste in this category.
  5. Whether anything is tracked past the form to assessment, start of care and hours.
  6. Caregiver capacity, because advertising for clients you can’t staff is a way to lose both.
