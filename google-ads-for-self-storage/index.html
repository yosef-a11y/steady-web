How storage is different

A storage facility isn't selling a product, it's filling a specific unit

Most agencies run a storage account the way they'd run one for any local service business: bid on "storage units near me," send everyone to the homepage, report cost per lead. That ignores the thing that actually determines whether the month was good, which is which sizes are sitting empty.

You don't need more renters in general. You need renters for the eleven 5x5s nobody has taken since spring, while the 10x10s have a waitlist. Those are different campaigns with different bids, and in most accounts they're the same campaign with one budget.

Structure around unit mix and vacancy

Searchers tell you what they want. Someone typing "10x20 storage unit" or "climate controlled storage" has already sized the job. So have the people searching for RV, boat and vehicle storage, which are usually your highest-rate spaces and your least-advertised.

Splitting campaigns by size and type lets you do the obvious thing: push budget at what's vacant, pull back on what's full, and stop paying for clicks on a size you can't rent anyway. When occupancy shifts, the budget shifts. Most accounts never move.

Radius, not city

People rent storage close to where they live or where they're moving to. A few miles in the wrong direction and they'll pick someone else, regardless of your rate. City-level targeting buys clicks from the far side of town that will never convert.

Multi-facility operators have the opposite problem: two locations bidding on the same searches, paying more to compete with themselves. Each facility needs its own geography, its own budget, and its own landing page showing that site's actual availability.

Defend your own name

Type your facility's name into Google and look at what's above the map. You'll often find aggregator listings and national operators bidding on it. A renter who searched for you specifically is the easiest move-in you'll ever get, and that's the one being intercepted.

Brand keywords cost very little, because nobody has a better quality score on your name than you do. The important part is reporting them separately. Brand and non-brand mixed into one number makes a mediocre account look fine, which is the most common way storage accounts hide their own problems.

Everyone runs the same promotion

First month free, or a dollar, is in nearly every ad in the category. It doesn't differentiate you, and it's priced into what renters expect. What does move the decision:

  • Whether the size they want is actually available, stated plainly.
  • Drive-up versus interior access, which matters more to renters than most operators assume.
  • Gate hours, especially for anyone storing work equipment.
  • Climate control, and what it costs relative to standard.
  • Online move-in, so a renter can finish at 10pm without calling you.

Rates in ad copy need care. If your street rates move with demand, an ad quoting a price from six weeks ago creates an argument at the counter.

Track move-ins, not reservations

Reservations are free to make and a real share of them never arrive. Optimize toward reservations and Google will reliably find you more people who enjoy making reservations.

The stages worth sending back as offline conversions:

  1. Reservation or inquiry submitted. The only thing most accounts track.
  2. Phone call connected and qualified. A large share of storage demand still calls.
  3. Move-in completed. The point where you have a tenant.
  4. Unit size and rate. Attach them, so the bidding learns that a 10x20 is worth more than a 5x5.

This also reframes what you can afford to pay. A tenant stays for months or years, and rate increases compound over that tenancy, so judging a channel on first-month revenue understates it badly. The right comparison is cost per move-in against the lifetime of the tenancy.

Seasonality is sharper than most operators budget for

Storage demand follows moving season. Summer is busy, deep winter is slow, and a flat monthly budget means you underspend when renters are everywhere and overspend when they aren't.

Pacing budget against your own occupancy curve — heavier when units open up, lighter when you're near full — is unglamorous and tends to be worth more than any bidding change.

Where storage accounts waste money

  • Broad match with thin negatives. "Storage" pulls in storage sheds, furniture, cloud storage and people looking for jobs.
  • Homepage landing pages. Someone who searched a specific size should land on that size, not on a site-wide rate table.
  • Performance Max absorbing brand. It will happily spend on people already searching your name and report them as new demand.
  • Untracked calls. If the phone is how people rent and the phone isn't tracked, the report is fiction.
  • Advertising full sizes. Paying for clicks on a unit you can't rent is pure loss, and it happens constantly.
