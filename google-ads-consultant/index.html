Working with a consultant

A Google Ads consultant is one accountable practitioner working inside your account, rather than a team working around it. That’s the whole difference, and it cuts both ways: you get the person who found the problem doing the work on it, with no account-manager layer between the diagnosis and the fix, and you give up the range and the cover that a larger team buys you. Which one is right depends less on budget than on whether your account needs breadth or judgment.

What a consultant does that an agency usually doesn’t

The job titles overlap enough to be meaningless, so here’s the practical distinction.

In most agency engagements the person you were sold is not the person who logs in. There’s a strategist on the call, a specialist in the account, and a coordinator between them, and the gap between what gets decided and what gets built is where accounts quietly drift. That structure buys real things — cover when someone is away, a designer when you need creative, somebody who has seen your exact problem before — but it costs directness.

A consultant engagement collapses that. The person who audits the account is the person who restructures it and the person who explains the result. Three consequences worth knowing about:

  • Advice and execution aren’t separable. You can ask why a decision was made and get the actual reason rather than a relayed one.
  • Scope is narrower by design. One person can run a serious Google Ads program. One person cannot also produce video, rebuild your site and run three other channels.
  • The engagement can end cleanly. A good consultant builds something your team can run, which is a different goal from a retainer that needs renewing.

When a consultant is the right call

Specific situations where this works better than hiring an agency:

  • You have someone in-house already. A marketer running the account who needs a second set of eyes and somebody to escalate to, not a replacement.
  • You need a diagnosis before committing a budget. Knowing what’s wrong is often worth more than another month of management, and it’s a much smaller commitment.
  • The account is a serious one but not a complicated one. Meaningful spend, one market, one channel. That’s a job for a competent person, not a department.
  • You’ve been through an agency and the work got juniorized. A common story: strong first ninety days, then the senior person moves to the next account.
  • You want it built and handed over. A defined project with an end, so your team owns it afterwards.

When it isn’t

Worth saying plainly, because the answer isn’t always us.

If the work spans several channels with real production attached — video, design, a content operation — you want a team, and stitching three freelancers together usually costs more than an agency and coordinates worse. If you’re running many markets or currencies, the account multiplies rather than grows and the hours stop fitting into one person. And if the business can’t tolerate a week of someone being unavailable, a single practitioner is a real risk that’s better named up front than discovered in August.

The diagnosis is usually the product

Most accounts that arrive aren’t short of management. They’re carrying two or three specific faults that nobody has gone looking for, and the monthly work has been running on top of them for a year.

The faults repeat across accounts with striking consistency: conversion tracking that records something other than what the business sells, brand and non-brand averaged into a single number that flatters both, budget pinned to campaigns that stopped deserving it, and bidding optimizing toward whatever was easiest to measure rather than what makes money. None of those is fixed by more hours a month. They’re fixed once, by someone who went and looked.

That’s why the audit comes first and why it’s free. If the findings are things your own team can action, that’s a good outcome and you should take them and go.

Consultant, freelancer, or agency

People search all three for the same need, and the labels are used loosely, so judge the engagement rather than the word.

What actually differs is what you’re buying. Hands means someone to execute a plan you already have, priced by time and interchangeable by design. Judgment means someone to decide what the plan should be and be accountable for it. A cheap engagement that turns out to be hands when you needed judgment is the most common way this goes wrong, and it isn’t detectable from the fee — it’s detectable from whether anyone asks about your margins before proposing a bid strategy.

The money side of this is covered separately in what Google Ads management costs, including how the three models price the same work differently and which red flags are worth walking away from.

How to judge one before you hire

  1. Ask what they’d look at first. A good answer starts with tracking and margin, not keywords. If the first move is a keyword list, they’re guessing.
  2. Ask who owns the account. You should, always. Leaving an engagement should never cost you your conversion history.
  3. Ask what they’d turn off. Anyone who only proposes additions isn’t reading the account, they’re selling work.
  4. Ask how they’d know it worked. The answer should name a business outcome, not a platform metric.
  5. Ask what they don’t do. Anybody who does everything does most of it badly.

Working alongside an in-house team

The most durable version of this isn’t replacement, it’s a split that plays to each side.

In-house holds the things that need to live in the business: the commercial decisions, the margin data, the product knowledge, the relationships with whoever fulfils the demand. An outside practitioner brings the pattern recognition that only comes from working across many accounts, plus the willingness to say something unwelcome, which is structurally harder for an employee.

Practically that looks like your team owning the account day to day and an outside pair of eyes on a defined rhythm, with the authority to escalate when something is wrong. It costs a fraction of full management and it catches the drift that nobody inside notices, because they see the account every day and change looks gradual from there.

What we look at first

  1. Whether the conversion data is true, compared against what the business actually recorded as revenue or closed work.
  2. Margin by product or service, because targets set without it are habit rather than math.
  3. Brand separated from non-brand, so the growth side can be judged honestly.
  4. Where the budget is versus where the return is, which are rarely the same place.
  5. What the search terms report says against what the business received.
  6. What should be switched off, which is usually the fastest money on the table.
