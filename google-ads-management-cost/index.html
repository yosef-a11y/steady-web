The models

Google Ads management is almost always billed one of three ways — a percentage of ad spend, a flat monthly retainer, or a base fee plus a share of results — and the choice matters less for what it costs than for what it rewards. Percentage pricing pays an agency more when your spend rises. A flat retainer pays the same whether they put four hours into the account or forty. Performance pricing pays for whatever metric you name, so the metric had better be the right one. The fee itself is usually the small number next to the spend it governs.

The three ways agencies charge

Almost every Google Ads agency uses one of three pricing structures. The differences aren't cosmetic. Each one points the agency's incentives somewhere, and it's worth knowing where before you sign.

1. Percentage of ad spend

Usually 10 to 20 percent of what you spend, often with a monthly minimum. It's common because it's simple and scales with account size.

What it rewards: larger budgets. An agency earning 15 percent makes more when your spend goes up, and earns nothing extra for telling you half of it is wasted. If you use this model, ask how the agency is paid when it recommends spending less.

2. Flat monthly retainer

A fixed retainer regardless of spend. For small and mid-size accounts this commonly runs between $1,000 and $5,000 a month, depending on complexity and market. Many agencies also charge a one-time onboarding or setup fee for the initial build, often somewhere between half and one full month's retainer.

What it rewards: efficiency. The retainer is the same whether the agency puts four hours or forty into your account, so get the scope of work in writing: what's included, how often, and what gets billed separately.

3. Performance-based

A base fee plus a share of results, or occasionally a straight cost per lead.

What it rewards: whatever metric you pick. Tie it to leads and you'll get leads, including poor ones. Tie it to revenue and you need attribution both sides trust. These arrangements work well when the measurement is solid and both parties agree on what counts.

What the fee should cover

Whatever the structure, a management fee should buy real work. At minimum:

  • Conversion tracking that's been verified, not assumed. If nobody has checked that the numbers are real, everything downstream is guesswork.
  • Ongoing search term review and negative keyword work, not just at setup.
  • Bid strategy and budget decisions made deliberately, with a reason you can hear.
  • Ad copy and creative testing that actually runs and reaches a conclusion.
  • Reporting you can read, in revenue and cost per acquisition rather than impressions and click-through rate.
  • Access to the person doing the work.

Questions to ask before you sign

  1. Who owns the ad account? If the agency owns it, leaving costs you your conversion history, which is the most valuable thing the account has built up.
  2. Is there a contract, and how long? Long minimum terms protect the agency from the consequences of poor performance. Ask about the setup fee and the notice period too.
  3. Who's in the account day to day? Not who's on the call. Who logs in.
  4. What happens if performance drops? A clear answer here tells you a lot.
  5. What are you optimizing toward, and how do you know it's accurate?

Why the cheapest option often isn't

Management fees are small compared to ad spend. On a $10,000 monthly budget, the difference between a $1,000 fee and a $2,000 fee is $1,000. A poorly structured account can waste several times that in the same month while the reporting still looks fine.

What the fee usually doesn't cover

Most disputes about price are really disputes about scope. These are the things commonly billed separately, and the ones worth naming in writing before you start:

  • Landing pages. Design, build and testing are often a separate line, and on a lead-gen account they matter more than the bidding.
  • Creative production. Image and video assets for Performance Max, Display and Meta — usually not included, and thin assets are a real performance problem.
  • Tracking implementation. Advising on conversion tracking is management. Building server-side tagging or wiring a CRM back into the ad account is a project.
  • Feed and Merchant Center work for e-commerce, which can be a standing job of its own rather than a task inside Shopping management.
  • Third-party subscriptions. Call tracking, feed tools and reporting platforms are usually passed through at cost, so ask which ones you'll be paying for.
  • Other channels. A Google Ads fee covers Google Ads. Meta, Microsoft or Amazon are separate scopes.

The fee as a share of total budget

The question behind most pricing searches isn't really what agencies charge, it's what proportion of the money goes to management rather than to clicks.

The mechanic is simple and it decides a lot. Management effort doesn't scale with spend. Auditing search terms, fixing tracking, writing ads and restructuring campaigns takes roughly the same work on a small account as on a large one, so the fee behaves like a fixed cost sitting on top of a variable one. As spend rises, the fee becomes a smaller share of the total. As spend falls, it becomes a larger one, and at some point it's most of the budget.

That's the honest reason agencies set minimums, and it's why the right question to ask isn't whether a fee is high or low in isolation. It's whether the total — fee plus spend — can buy enough volume for the account to learn from and still leave margin. A budget that goes almost entirely to management buys advice rather than customers, and a budget with no management behind it usually funds the same mistakes month after month.

What makes one account cost more than another

Two businesses spending the same amount can be very different jobs. What drives a quote up:

  • A product feed. Shopping and Performance Max mean Merchant Center health, feed quality and disapprovals as ongoing work rather than setup.
  • Multiple locations or service areas, each needing its own geography, budget and landing page so they don't bid against each other.
  • More than one country or currency, which multiplies the account rather than adding to it.
  • A long sales cycle. When the real conversion happens weeks later in a CRM, somebody has to build and maintain the loop that feeds it back.
  • Phone-led businesses, where call tracking and call quality review are part of the job.
  • Regulated categories, where ad copy needs compliance review and policy restrictions shape how the account can be built.
  • The number of people who need to agree. Reporting and meetings are real hours.

Agency, freelancer, or in-house

The three cost structures aren't comparable on fee alone.

A freelancer is usually the lowest fee and the narrowest bus factor: you get one person's attention and one person's blind spots, and holidays and illness are your problem too.

An agency costs more and buys range — someone who has seen feeds break, someone who writes, someone who builds tracking — plus continuity when a person leaves. The risk is being sold a senior team and handed a junior one, which is why it's worth asking who actually logs in.

In-house looks cheapest at scale and rarely is at the start. A competent practitioner's salary, plus tools, plus the months before they're useful, plus the fact that one person in-house sees only your account and loses the pattern recognition that comes from working across many. It makes sense when spend is large enough that a dedicated person is fully occupied by it.

Many businesses end up with a hybrid: someone in-house who owns the commercial decisions, and an outside party for the execution and the second opinion.

Pricing red flags

  • The agency owns the ad account. Leaving costs you the conversion history, which is the most valuable thing the account has.
  • A percentage with no ceiling. Fine at small scale, uncomfortable at large, and it quietly rewards the one recommendation you can't audit.
  • Long minimum terms with no performance clause. A twelve-month lock protects the agency from the consequences of its own work.
  • Ad spend presented as a result. If the report leads with budget deployed rather than what it produced, that's a tell.
  • A guarantee. Nobody controls the auction, and a guaranteed position or lead count usually means the definition is doing the work.
  • No named person. If nobody will say who logs in, the answer is usually several people, briefly.

If you're the one setting the price

A fair number of people searching this are freelancers and small agencies working out what to charge rather than what to pay. The useful principle is the same from either side: price the work, not the spend.

Spend is a poor proxy for effort. A single-campaign account at high spend can be less work than a multi-location account at a fraction of it, so pricing purely off budget means overcharging simple accounts and losing money on complex ones. Scope the hours the account genuinely needs — including the unbilled ones nobody counts, like the audit before onboarding, the reporting, and the month something breaks — and set a floor beneath which the account isn't worth taking.

The floor matters more than the rate. Most underpriced engagements aren't underpriced because the hourly figure was wrong; they're underpriced because the account turned out to need three times the attention and the fee had nowhere to go.
