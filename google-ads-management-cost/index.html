The models

The three ways agencies charge, and what each one rewards

Almost every Google Ads agency uses one of three structures. The differences are not cosmetic: each one points the agency's incentives somewhere, and it is worth knowing where before you sign.

1. Percentage of ad spend

Typically 10–20% of what you spend, often with a monthly minimum. Common because it is simple and scales with account size.

What it rewards: spending more. That is not an accusation of bad faith, it is just arithmetic. An agency on 15% earns more when your budget goes up, and earns nothing extra for telling you that half your spend is wasted. If you use this model, ask directly how the agency is compensated when it recommends a budget cut.

2. Flat monthly fee

A fixed figure regardless of spend. For small to mid-size accounts this commonly runs somewhere between $1,000 and $5,000 a month, varying widely with complexity and market.

What it rewards: efficiency, and at the margin, doing less. The agency keeps the same fee whether it spends four hours or forty on your account. The protection is specificity: what is actually included, how often, and what counts as out of scope.

3. Performance-based

A base fee plus a share of results, or in rarer cases a pure cost-per-lead arrangement.

What it rewards: whatever metric you picked, which is the catch. Tie it to leads and you will get leads, including bad ones. Tie it to revenue and you need attribution both sides trust, which is harder than it sounds. These arrangements work when the measurement is genuinely sound and both parties agree what counts. They go wrong when it is not.

What you should be getting for the fee

Whatever the structure, a management fee should buy actual work. At minimum:

  • Conversion tracking that is verified, not assumed. If nobody has checked that the numbers in the dashboard are real, everything downstream is decoration.
  • Search term review and negative keyword work on an ongoing basis, not once at setup.
  • Bid strategy and budget decisions made deliberately, with a reason you can hear.
  • Ad copy and creative testing that actually runs and concludes.
  • Reporting you can read, in revenue and cost per acquisition rather than impressions and click-through rate.
  • Access to the person doing the work.

Five things worth asking before you sign

  1. Who owns the ad account? If the answer is the agency, leaving costs you your conversion history, which is the asset the account has been accumulating.
  2. Is there a contract, and how long? Long minimum terms protect the agency from the consequences of poor performance.
  3. Who is in the account day to day? Not who is on the call. Who logs in.
  4. What happens if performance drops? A straight answer here tells you a lot.
  5. What are you optimizing toward, and how do you know it is accurate?

Why the cheapest option usually is not

Management fees are small next to ad spend. On a $10,000 monthly budget, the difference between a $1,000 fee and a $2,000 fee is $1,000. A poorly structured account can waste several times that in a month, quietly, while the reporting still looks fine. The fee is rarely where the money is won or lost.
