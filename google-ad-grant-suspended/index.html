Ad Grants · suspension

A suspended Google Ad Grant is recoverable, and the thing that most often delays recovery is appealing before fixing anything. Suspension is a policy outcome with a specific cause behind it — usually the account-wide click-through floor, conversion tracking that stopped recording, keywords that were never eligible, or a compliance step that lapsed. Find the cause, fix it at source, prune what caused the drift, and request review once. An account that goes back in unchanged usually comes back out the same way, and each failed appeal makes the next one harder.

First: is it actually suspended?

Several different things look identical from the dashboard, and they need completely different responses.

  • The Ad Grants account is suspended. The grant itself has been pulled for a policy or performance reason. This is the one this page is about.
  • Ads are disapproved but the account is fine. Individual ads or keywords rejected on policy grounds. Routine, and fixed at the ad level.
  • Campaigns are paused or out of budget. Worth ruling out before anything else, and it happens more often than anyone admits.
  • Google for Nonprofits eligibility has lapsed. The grant sits on top of that enrollment. If the organization's status or verification has expired, the grant goes with it, and no amount of account work fixes it — the enrollment has to be sorted first.
  • A required compliance step was missed. The program has periodic requirements, including an annual survey. Missing one suspends an otherwise healthy account, and it's the easiest cause to fix once identified.

The notification Google sends names a reason. It's terse, but it's the fastest way to tell which of these you're in, and it determines everything that follows.

What actually trips a grant account

In rough order of how often it's the culprit:

The account-wide click-through floor. Grant accounts have to maintain a minimum click-through rate measured across the whole account, not per campaign. This is the cause most often, and it's nearly always self-inflicted in a particular way: the allowance felt like money going to waste, so broad and loosely related keywords were added to spend more of it. Those terms collect impressions and almost no clicks, the account average sinks, and the effort to use the grant is what breaks it.

Conversion tracking that stopped recording. The program expects meaningful conversions, and automated bidding — the only route past the grant's bid cap — can't function without them. A tag that broke during a site migration can therefore take down compliance and performance at the same time, months before anyone notices, because nothing in the interface announces that conversions went quiet.

Keywords that were never eligible. Single words, overly generic terms, and keywords below the program's quality threshold. These often date to the original build and sit there until something draws attention to them.

Missing or wrong geographic targeting. Grant campaigns are required to target locations, and an inherited campaign with targeting switched off is a quiet violation.

Site and landing page policy. Ads have to lead to the organization's own domain, and the site has to match what the program expects of a nonprofit. A site that has drifted toward looking like a commercial operation, or that sends traffic somewhere else, becomes a problem.

Google publishes the current requirements and the thresholds move, so confirm the specifics against their own documentation rather than any article — this one included — before building a remediation plan around them.

Why appealing first makes it worse

The instinct is to request review immediately, because the account is down and every day costs traffic. It's the wrong move.

A review looks at the account as it currently stands. If nothing has changed since the suspension, the same conditions are still there and the same answer comes back. Worse, repeated requests on an unchanged account make the next one slower and less likely to be read generously.

The cost of waiting two days to fix the cause properly is two days. The cost of three failed appeals is weeks, plus a harder conversation at the end of it.

The sequence that works

  1. Read the notification and confirm the category. Policy, performance, or eligibility — they diverge immediately from here.
  2. Find the cause in the data, not by guessing. For a click-through suspension, that means the keyword and search-term reports: which terms carried impressions and produced nothing.
  3. Fix it at source. Remove the terms rather than pausing them. Repair the tracking and confirm it records a real conversion before moving on. Restore geographic targeting. Resolve the enrollment or survey if that's the cause.
  4. Prune harder than feels comfortable. Everything that can't justify itself goes. The account that comes back should be noticeably smaller.
  5. Verify conversions are recording, with a live test, not an assumption. This is the step most often skipped and the one that causes a second suspension.
  6. Request review once, and say what changed.

Expect it to come back smaller

The account that gets reinstated is usually spending a fraction of the allowance on a much tighter set of terms, and performing better than the one that was suspended. That isn't a consolation prize, it's the actual lesson.

The allowance is a ceiling, not a target. A grant account spending modestly on genuinely relevant searches is working correctly; one spending far more on vague ones is on borrowed time whether or not it has been suspended yet. That principle, and the rest of how these accounts differ from paid ones, is set out on the main Google Ads and the Ad Grant for nonprofits page.

Keeping it from happening again

Suspension is rarely a surprise in hindsight. What prevents a repeat is dull and specific:

  • Watch click-through rate as an account-level number, on a schedule. Campaign averages hide the problem, because one bad campaign drags the account figure that actually matters.
  • Alert on conversions going quiet. A week of zeroes after a site change should reach a human. This single check prevents the most expensive version of the problem.
  • Calendar the compliance steps, including the annual survey, rather than relying on an email reaching the right person.
  • Review the keyword list quarterly and remove rather than pause.
  • Treat a new campaign as a risk to the account average, not just to its own budget.

If it doesn't come back

Occasionally reinstatement doesn't happen, usually where the underlying eligibility is the issue rather than the account.

That's survivable, and worth planning for before it's urgent. The traffic a grant account was producing was mostly people looking for the services the organization provides and ways to volunteer, and a modest paid budget reaches the same searches without the bid cap or the performance requirements. The email list built while the grant was running keeps working either way, which is the argument for building it while things are going well rather than after they aren't.

What we look at first

  1. The suspension notice itself, which names a category most people skip past.
  2. Whether Google for Nonprofits enrollment is current, before touching the ad account at all.
  3. Account-level click-through rate over time, to see when the drift started and what was added around then.
  4. Whether conversions are recording, tested rather than assumed.
  5. The keyword list against the program's rules, for terms that were never eligible.
  6. Geographic targeting on every campaign, including inherited ones.
